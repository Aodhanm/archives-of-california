What is in the Archives of California

The government archive of Spanish and Mexican California, what survives of it, and what it can tell you.

The Archives of California are the working papers of the government of California before the American conquest: the letters, orders, musters, censuses, court cases, land papers and accounts that passed between a governor at Monterey, his presidio commanders, the mission priests, the town councils, and the viceroy in Mexico City. They run from the founding expedition of 1769 to the eve of statehood.

What happened to them

The originals burned. They were held in San Francisco and destroyed in the fire of 1906, which is the central fact about this collection and the reason it is unusual. What survives is the copy made in the 1870s, when Hubert Howe Bancroft sent Thomas Savage and a team of copyists into the state archive to transcribe and abstract it for his History of California. Savage's copies, bound as BANC MSS C-A, are now at the Bancroft Library at Berkeley, and for most of this material they are the only record there is.

That gives the collection a double character. It is a primary source, because the documents are faithfully reported, and it is also a nineteenth-century editorial artefact, because a copyist chose what to transcribe in full, what to abstract, and what to skip. Serious use means keeping both facts in view at once.

How it is organised

The archive is not one run of volumes but a set of series, each reflecting the office that generated or received the papers. The main ones:

Provincial State Papers (C-A 1 to 14)

The Spanish-era core: the governor's incoming correspondence with the viceroy, the Commandant General of the Internal Provinces, and his own officers. Founding orders, supply and payroll, Indian policy, defence, mission business. If you want the eighteenth century, you want this series.

Provincial and Departmental State Papers, Benicia (C-A 15 to 20, 38 to 42)

Military and fiscal papers that went to Benicia: presidial company musters and pay, artillery and munitions returns, the customs house, the commissary and treasury. This is where a question about who held a post, how many men were present, and whether they were paid gets answered.

Provincial Records and Departmental Records (C-A 22 to 26, 46 to 49)

The other direction of the correspondence: the governor's own outgoing letter-books and order-books. Where the State Papers show what reached him, the Records show what he ordered.

Departmental State Papers by town (C-A 34 to 37 Los Ángeles, 43 Monterey, 44 and 45 San José)

Local administration in the Mexican period: alcaldes and ayuntamientos, land and cattle disputes, criminal cases, elections, schools. The closest thing in the collection to ordinary life.

State Papers, Missions and Colonization (C-A 50 to 53)

Mission administration, the secularization programme of the 1830s, inventories, and the colonization schemes that followed.

Legislative Records (C-A 59 to 61)

The territorial assembly, the Diputación: its sessions, decrees, and the acts of a body that for the first time could check the governor.

The volume guide lists all sixty-three with their series, tomos, dates and record counts; how to cite gives the full series table in citation form.

What it can actually answer

The collection rewards specific questions better than general ones. Some it answers unusually well:

Who was where, and when. Musters, rosters and pay lists make it possible to place a soldier at a presidio in a given month, and to watch a garrison shrink. In 1833 one muster found thirty-five men present in all of California.
What a government actually did day to day. Not policy in the abstract but the order to repair a church roof, the licence to fetch wine for Mass, the price fixed for maize, the search for a runaway.
How many people there were. Padrones and estados count the population settlement by settlement, in the categories the state itself used. At independence in 1822 the whole province held 24,990 people, of whom 21,196 were mission neophytes.
Relations with Native peoples, from the colonial side. Campaigns, captures, mission flight, negotiated peaces, and the occasional order forbidding force. The record is one-sided by construction, and reading it well means reading against it.
Foreign contact. Russian hunting parties, British and American ships, the Nootka crisis, Fort Ross, and the long anxiety about who else wanted this coast.

What it is weak on

It is a government archive, so it sees what governments see. Native voices appear mediated through Spanish officials; women appear mainly in court cases and censuses; the mission interior is better documented in church registers elsewhere. And because Savage abstracted rather than transcribed much of it, the exact wording of a given document often cannot be recovered from the copy at all.

Start here if you are new: search the main catalogue for a place, a person or a year, then open any record to see its summary, its dates, both page systems, and a link to the manuscript leaf. For context on how the government worked, see How California Was Governed and The Governors of California.

What this site adds

Until recently there was no item-level index to the collection: you could learn that C-A 8 existed, but not what document 148 in it was. This catalogue describes every document in all sixty-three volumes, with dates, authors, recipients, places, both page systems, and a link to the exact manuscript leaf. It is free and openly licensed, it publishes its own errata register, it states a measured accuracy figure rather than claiming perfection, and every record page invites corrections.
